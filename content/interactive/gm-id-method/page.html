The gm/ID method

01Why the square law lets you down

Most of us learn to size transistors with the long-channel square law, ID = ½ μCox(W/L)(VGS−VT)². It gives tidy hand equations, and it is wrong in exactly the places modern analog design lives.

Low-power circuits bias devices with small overdrive, often below threshold. There the current is exponential in VGS, so the square law predicts a transconductance that runs off to infinity as VOV → 0. Short-channel devices add velocity saturation and mobility degradation, so even in strong inversion the hand numbers can be off by a factor of two. You end up guessing, simulating, and adjusting W until it works.

The gm/ID method, introduced by Silveira, Flandre and Jespers in 1996, replaces the model with measured (simulated) device data. You still design by hand, but against curves from your actual process, indexed by a variable that behaves well in every region of operation.

02What gm/ID really is: a log slope

Write the definition a slightly different way:

gm/ID = (1/ID)·(∂ID/∂VGS) = ∂(ln ID)/∂VGS

Plot the drain current on a log axis against VGS. The slope of that curve is gm/ID (times ln 10 if you use log10). Below threshold the current is exponential, so the log plot is a straight line and the slope is at its maximum. As the device turns on harder, the curve bends over and the slope falls. Drag across the chart to see this.

Two intuitions fall out of this picture.

It is an efficiency. Gain, bandwidth and noise all depend on gm. Power depends on ID. So gm/ID tells you how much useful transconductance each microamp buys. A device at 25 V⁻¹ gives you more than three times the gm of one at 7 V⁻¹ for the same current.

It is the overdrive that still works below threshold. In strong inversion gm/ID = 2/VOV, so every designer's intuition for VOV carries over: 10 V⁻¹ is about 200 mV of overdrive, 5 V⁻¹ about 400 mV. Unlike VOV, though, gm/ID stays finite and accurate as you go into moderate and weak inversion.

03The three inversion regions

A cleaner horizontal axis than VGS is the inversion coefficient, IC = ID / Ispec, where Ispec = 2nμCoxUT²·(W/L) is the current at which the device sits right in the middle of moderate inversion. IC strips out size and threshold, so every transistor in every process traces out nearly the same gm/ID curve:

Weak inversion

IC < 0.1 · gm/ID ≈ 22–30 V⁻¹

Diffusion current, exponential I–V, BJT-like. Maximum gm per amp, tiny current density, so wide devices with large capacitance and low fT. Home of low-power sensors and biomedical front ends.

Moderate inversion

0.1 < IC < 10 · gm/ID ≈ 10–22 V⁻¹

Neither diffusion nor drift dominates. No simple hand formula fits, which is why the square-law approach struggles here. Often the best compromise between efficiency and speed.

Strong inversion

IC > 10 · gm/ID ≈ 3–10 V⁻¹

Drift current, square-law-ish (then velocity-limited at short L). High current density, small devices, high fT, but each microamp buys little gm. Used for speed, matching and large signal swing per device.

04The trade-off: efficiency against speed

Increase gm/ID and you push the device toward weak inversion. You get more gm per amp, but the current density drops, so for a given current the device must be wider. Wider means more gate capacitance per unit of gm, so the transit frequency fT = gm/(2πCgg) falls. Channel length L is the second knob: longer L raises intrinsic gain gm/gds but costs fT roughly as 1/L².

05The trick that makes it work: width drops out

Think of a wide transistor as many narrow unit transistors in parallel. Double W and you double ID, gm, gds and every capacitance, at the same bias voltages. Ratios of these quantities do not change. So:

gm/ID,   ID/W,   fT = gm/2πCgg,   gm/gds   →   functions of (VGS, VDS, L) only

Because gm/ID is a one-to-one function of VGS (at a given L), you can use it as the index instead. Now a transistor is described by two design knobs, gm/ID and L, which set all of its quality (speed, gain, density, headroom), plus one scale factor, the current ID (or equivalently W), which sets its quantity.

That separation is the whole method. You choose quality per device from its role in the circuit, the specs fix how much gm you need, and the width simply follows: W = ID / (ID/W), with ID/W read from a lookup table.

The lookup tables come from your process design kit. Simulate one unit-width NMOS and PMOS, sweep VGS, VDS, L (and VSB if you care), and save gm, gds, ID and the capacitances. Because the data come from the foundry's own models, short-channel effects are included automatically, which no hand equation can match.

06The design procedure

  1. Translate specs into required gm

    Bandwidth, gain-bandwidth, noise and settling usually set a transconductance. For a single-stage OTA, gm = 2π·GBW·CL. For noise, input-referred thermal noise ≈ 4kTγ/gm.

  2. Choose gm/ID for each device from its role

    Input pairs that need low power and noise go high (15–25). Devices that must be fast, match well or keep a small VDsat trade-off go low (5–10). See section 8.

  3. Choose L

    Longer L for intrinsic gain and lower flicker noise and mismatch; shorter L for fT. Check gain and fT on the (gm/ID, L) curves.

  4. Compute the current

    ID = gm / (gm/ID). This is your power budget for the device.

  5. Compute the width

    Read ID/W at (gm/ID, L) from the table; W = ID / (ID/W). Also read VGS and capacitances for biasing and parasitic estimates.

  6. Verify and iterate in simulation

    Check headroom, fT margin over signal frequency (aim for 10× or more), parasitic poles from the device's own Cgg, then simulate. Results usually land within a few percent because the tables came from the same models.

07Worked example: the input pair of a 5-transistor OTA

Spec: gain-bandwidth 50 MHz driving a 1 pF load. In a single-stage OTA the unity-gain frequency is gm1/(2πCL), so the input pair needs gm1 = 2π × 50 MHz × 1 pF ≈ 314 µS.

The interesting decision is gm/ID. Try the presets: at 8 V⁻¹ the pair is small and very fast but burns current; at 22 V⁻¹ the current is tiny but the device grows wide, and its gate capacitance starts to load the previous stage.

Reading the result

At the balanced choice (15 V⁻¹, L = 0.5 µm) each side needs about 21 µA and roughly 9 µm of width, with fT more than 30× above GBW. That margin matters because the non-dominant pole at the mirror node sits near gm3/Cmirror, and the input pair's own gate capacitance loads whatever drives it.

The remaining devices follow the same recipe. The PMOS mirror M3/M4 is usually set to a lower gm/ID (around 8–10) for better current matching and a higher mirror pole; it carries the same ID, so its width comes straight from the PMOS table. The tail M5 carries 2ID; a low gm/ID gives it a compact layout and good matching with its bias reference, at the cost of a larger VDsat eating into common-mode range.

08Choosing gm/ID by device role

These ranges are starting points for bulk CMOS. Treat them as a first guess, then let the specs move you.

Device roleTypical gm/IDWhy
High-speed / RF devices, wideband buffers5 – 8 V⁻¹Maximises fT; keeps devices and parasitics small.
Current mirrors, current sources, tail devices5 – 10 V⁻¹Current mismatch scales as σ(ΔI/I) ≈ (gm/ID)·σ(VT), so low values match better. Also lowers the noise current they inject (gm is smaller for the same ID).
General OTA input pairs, gain stages10 – 15 V⁻¹Near the peak of gm/ID·fT: a good balance of power and speed.
Low-power, low-noise input pairs (sensors, biomedical, ULP)18 – 25 V⁻¹Maximises gm per µA, so minimum thermal noise per unit power; large area also lowers flicker noise and offset.
Cascode devices10 – 20 V⁻¹High gm/ID keeps VDsat small, saving headroom; watch the pole their capacitance adds.

Rules of thumb that fall out of the curves

Headroom: VDsat ≈ 2/(gm/ID). At 20 V⁻¹ a device needs only ~100 mV across it to stay saturated, which is why high gm/ID suits low-voltage design.

Noise: for fixed current, input-referred thermal noise power falls as 1/(gm/ID). Doubling gm/ID halves the noise power without spending another microamp.

Diminishing returns: past ~22 V⁻¹ the curve flattens toward its ceiling. Going from 22 to 26 gains about 18% efficiency but can cost several times the width and capacitance. The last few V⁻¹ are rarely worth it.

09Practical pitfalls

Use the right VDS and VSB. Tables are usually generated at VDS = VDD/2 and VSB = 0. gds in particular changes a lot with VDS, and source-follower or stacked devices see body effect. Generate tables at the operating point you expect, or interpolate in VDS.

Use the same L you looked up. Every figure of merit depends strongly on L. Mixing a table at 0.5 µm with a layout at 0.35 µm throws the numbers off.

Include extrinsic capacitance. fT from Cgg is the device's own limit. Junction caps, overlap and wiring add to it; at short L overlap capacitance can be a large fraction of the total.

Check minimum width and fingers. Very low currents at low gm/ID can produce widths below the process minimum, while very high gm/ID can produce widths that need many fingers. Either is a hint to revisit the choice.
